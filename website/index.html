Real app, demo data.

I frankensteined yazio, fddb, MyFitnessPal and Excel into my own meal planner.

I have a full-time job, a young family and fitness goals I take seriously. Every tracker I tried, fddb, yazio, MyFitnessPal, is built around logging what you already ate. I wanted to decide on Sunday what I eat all week, and know it adds up.

A spreadsheet got me halfway. It did the maths, but it was never ergonomic enough to keep up every week. So I took what worked in each and stitched it into forkcast, for myself, with no plan to turn it into a product.

It has grown to the point where other people might want it too. This page is how I find out whether that's true, and whether you'd want it hosted or on your own server.

How it works

The week is the unit. Daily logging is still there, as the flexible part, not the main job.

Plan the week

Put meals on each day of the coming week and see the daily and weekly averages against your goals before you've eaten anything. Copy a day to the next when you meal-prep.

  • Protein
  • Carbs
  • Fat

Adjust the day

Plans change. Swap a meal or log something unplanned on your phone, and the macros update while you type the amount.

Shop from the plan

The grocery list adds up everything the week needs, with piece hints like "≈ 3" for onions. Set how many servings of each recipe you'll actually cook, and the amounts follow. Tick off what's already at home, or send the list to Bring!.

Cook once for the week

Cook a planned recipe in one go for every meal it has that week. The cooking view shows the amounts for the whole pot, the steps and the macros each serving logs. Used the whole bottle, or swapped an ingredient? Change it while you cook, and every planned meal follows. The screen stays on.

Two things that save the most typing

Most of the time in a food tracker goes into entering food. These two cut it down.

Packaged foods via Open Food Facts

Search branded products or scan a barcode, and the nutrition values come from Open Food Facts. If a product isn't there, photograph the packaging and it's read into your own catalog.

Recipes from photos

Photograph a cookbook page, take a screenshot or shoot both sides of a recipe card, and you get a draft recipe. You check every ingredient before anything is saved, and the recipe then carries its macros per portion.

And the rest of the basics

Your own food catalog
A curated base of everyday foods you can correct and extend, with piece weights like "1 egg".
Your goals, no diet template
Set calories and macros yourself, or work them out with the built-in calculator.
Phone first, desktop too
An installable web app that works offline in the supermarket and is just as usable at a desk.
Weight alongside the plan
Log your weight and see the trend next to what you actually planned and ate.

How it's built, and where data goes

Right now forkcast runs as one instance for one person: me. Here is everything it touches.

  • Meals, recipes, goals Plain JSON files on the server that runs it. No database, no account system.
  • Captured products Products read from packaging photos are stored in your own food catalog, on your server.
  • Product search Queries Open Food Facts when you switch it on or scan a barcode.
  • Photo import Recipe and packaging photos go to the Anthropic API, only if you configure your own API key. Without one, the feature hides itself.

Questions

Why not just use a spreadsheet?

I tried, in Excel. It handled the maths, but entering a week of meals, looking up macros and rebuilding the grocery list was never ergonomic enough to do every single week. forkcast exists because the spreadsheet kept getting skipped.

Is there a hosted version?

Not yet. Whether there will be one depends on the answers to the question at the top of this page. If you left your e-mail on the Tally form, you'll hear about it first.

Will it cost money?

The code is public and you can run it yourself. Whether a hosted version would cost something isn't decided. There's nothing to sell yet.

Where is my data?

When you self-host: in JSON files on your server. Product lookups go to Open Food Facts, and photos go to the Anthropic API only if you add a key. See "How it's built" above for the full list.

Is the app available in English?

Yes. The app speaks English and German: it follows your browser's language, and you can switch it in the settings. The food catalog has English names too.

Who's behind this?

One developer, building it in spare time for their own kitchen. No company, no investors, no growth team.
